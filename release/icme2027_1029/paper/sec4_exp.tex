\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

\subsection{Dataset and evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:data}
\textbf{Acquisition.}
A fixed camera records reflection-contaminated images through
automotive tinted glass. The camera side is then covered with black
velvet to obtain the paired transmission reference. The benchmark
contains 631 pairs over 13 training scenes and separate held-out
captures. Four attenuation conditions comprise the unmodified
image and adjustments of $8/11/14\%$. We retain the signed
image-domain residual and candidate occlusion annotations.
The 15-scene evaluation panel is fixed across the model
comparisons. The protocol also has limitations: reference frames
can be temporally displaced from the inputs, and covered captures
can change illumination. We report these rather than assuming
perfectly identical acquisition conditions.

We evaluate PSNR, SSIM, high-frequency PSNR, and gradient
agreement where available. The paired panel has a reported
minimum detectable PSNR difference of $0.26$~dB;
brightness-only effects require approximately
$1.19$--$1.31$~dB under the reported protocol.
These values characterize this evaluation, not a universal
significance threshold.

\subsection{Zero-shot benchmark}
\label{sec:bench}
We evaluate six public methods without training on our
tinted-glass images. RRW attains the highest PSNR
($20.50$~dB), followed by DSRNet ($20.25$~dB) and
RDNet ($20.05$~dB). The identity output gives
$18.44$~dB. Of the reported public methods,
IBCLN ($18.12$~dB) falls below identity, whereas
ERRNet ($18.90$~dB) does not. This ranking illustrates
domain-dependent performance but cannot, on its own,
establish whether synthetic or real training is preferable.

Regional scores must also be interpreted against the
correct baseline. The v13 evaluation partition uses
a reflection-related criterion and is distinct from
the camera-side occlusion region $\Omega$. Table~
\ref{tab:regiongain} reports both partition scores
and the improvement over identity on those same pixels.

All six methods gain more over identity within the
v13-selected region than outside it. However, identity
itself scores $3.81$~dB lower inside the partition.
A negative difference between raw inside/outside
scores is therefore not evidence of occlusion-specific
failure. Moreover, the training-time occlusion
criterion expands to a median $35\%$ of pixels on
these outdoor test scenes, compared with about
$15$--$21\%$ in the training pools. We consequently
do not interpret the v13 score as performance inside
$\Omega$. Instead, we evaluate occlusion supervision
during controlled training.

\subsection{Supervised transfer}
\label{sec:ft}
RDNet is fine-tuned with Mona adapters, a frozen
FocalNet backbone, and $65.95$~M trainable parameters.
The matched full-pool arms use 6000 iterations,
batch size 6, learning rate $10^{-4}$, and
fixed proportions of real and synthetic sources.
The principal controlled variable is which
acquisition months supply the real pairs. We
distinguish these experiments from earlier
small-pool single-seed runs.

The may+jun pool achieves $22.70$~dB, exceeding
the strongest evaluated public zero-shot model
by $2.22$~dB and improving all 15 scenes
(reported scene-level $95\%$ interval:
$[+1.73,+2.72]$~dB). The matched apr+jun
pool reaches $20.97$~dB, a $0.47$~dB gain
with interval $[-0.03,+0.93]$~dB. We
do not treat the latter as a reliable
improvement.

Removing the region-normalized loss from
the corresponding fine-tuning runs reduces
apr+jun from $20.96$ to $17.77$~dB and
may+jun from $22.71$ to $18.33$~dB.
The loss formulation is therefore important
under this recipe. This ablation does not
isolate the contribution of the true
occlusion \emph{location}, which we
investigate separately.

A per-image affine transform fitted to the
reference after inference raises apr+jun
from $20.97$ to $22.13$~dB and may+jun
from $22.70$ to $23.14$~dB. The $1.01$~dB
difference after this target-dependent
correction suggests that the pool gap is
not only a brightness mismatch. This
affine fit is a diagnostic upper bound,
not an image-only processing step.
Replacing reconstruction with an
affine-invariant objective did not
yield a consistent gain (two seeds:
$20.46$ and $17.77$~dB, versus a
$20.96$~dB baseline).

The better pool contains fewer
occlusion examples; its improvement
should therefore be attributed to
the tested domain-adaptation
configuration, not to the
availability of occlusion masks.

\label{sec:diag}

the region-normalized objective.
The primary arms use the same 82
visually confirmed training pairs
and three seeds per setting.
Additional single-seed experiments
are identified in Table~\ref{tab:placement}.

Removing the region term gives
$17.76$~dB. Supervision on the
true occlusion mask gives
$19.41$~dB, whereas an
area-matched random disk gives
$19.90$~dB. The corresponding
seed standard deviations are
$0.54$ and $0.16$~dB.
Thus the tested loss benefits
restoration, but the data do
not establish a further advantage
from its precise occlusion
placement.

The exploratory whole-image
relative-error arm scores
$20.05$~dB, and a disk fixed
per pair scores $20.25$~dB
in the reported single-seed
run. The result points toward
normalization by estimated
reflection amplitude as a
plausible source of the gain,
but this hypothesis needs a
matched multi-seed comparison.
Providing the observed in-region
darkening gives $20.06$~dB
in another screening run.

A thin-boundary regularizer
changes PSNR by $+0.72$,
$-0.37$, and $-0.17$~dB
over three seeds (mean
about $+0.06$~dB), without
a consistent benefit.
Equation~\eqref{eq:bound}
limits direct improvements
confined to a narrow
boundary; it does not
bound image-wide changes
induced during training.

\subsection{Reflection-location supervision}
\label{sec:gate}
A gradient-comparison pseudo-label
$G_I>G_T$ occupies $48.1\%$
of pixels on our data and
achieves IoU $0.063$ against
the reflection-energy target.
Supervising the same head
using a location target derived
from the signed reflection
layer raises its IoU to
$0.376$ (precision $0.739$,
recall $0.489$) with the
same image-only test interface.

However, the more accurate
location map does not improve
restoration when used as a
pixelwise loss weight:
$19.75$~dB for the
reflection-derived label
versus $20.36$~dB for
the gradient-comparison
label. The unmasked
relative-error arm obtains
$20.05$~dB in its
separate screening run.
These results apply to
loss weighting and do
not assess the learned
map as a conditioning
input.

\subsection{Photometric error and conditioning}
\label{sec:photom}
To examine global appearance
separately from spatial restoration,
we fit a post-hoc scalar or
affine correction to each
reference target. These
oracle corrections are not
available at test time and
serve only to measure
photometric sensitivity.

On the April subset, affine-adjusted
identity scores $20.10$~dB,
compared with $20.40$~dB
for the adjusted official model.
On May, the corresponding
scores are $25.77$ and
$25.44$~dB. These
diagnostics show that
photometric mismatch accounts
for substantial error on
these subsets without
establishing that spatial
restoration is unnecessary
in general.

Across 35 fine-tuned arms
evaluated on a 12-scene
held-out subset, mean
brightness bias is
$6.66$ gray levels.
A post-hoc scalar gain
$g=\mu(T)/\mu(\hat T)$
raises PSNR by $0.63$~dB
on average (maximum
$1.89$~dB). The
single-image scale
ambiguity therefore
has a measurable
counterpart in the
reported predictions.

Finally, we perturb an explicit
conditioning scalar $c$
while holding the input fixed,
and measure
\[
\mathrm{ControlGain}
=\frac{\partial\mu(\hat T)/\partial c}
{\mu(I)}.
\]
Our experimental acceptance
criterion is a value of at
least $0.50$. A frozen
conditioned model scores
$0.020$, an absorption-aware
model $0.086$, and a model
trained with counterfactually
sampled conditions $0.244$;
the uninformative-condition
control scores $0.020$.
Counterfactual training improves
responsiveness, but no
tested model meets the
chosen threshold. This
intervention measures
whether the condition
changes the prediction;
it does not make the
absolute target scale
identifiable from the
input image.
