\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Single-image reflection removal (SIRR) aims to recover the scene transmitted through a
reflecting surface from a single photograph. Even under the simplified additive model
$I=T+R$, one observation must determine two overlapping layers. Learning-based methods
therefore rely heavily on training pairs and on the assumptions used to construct them.
Recent architectures have improved layer separation through iterative refinement,
cross-layer interaction, and learned image priors~\cite{li2020ibcln,hu2023dsrnet,zhao2025rdnet}.
At the same time, real-pair datasets have become central to evaluation and adaptation,
particularly when synthetic reflections fail to reproduce real imaging
conditions~\cite{zhu2024rrw,openrr2025,cai2026ntire}.

A paired reference is not, however, a direct measurement of an abstract reflection-free
scene. It is an image acquired under a specific procedure. Removing the glass changes
the optical path; capturing an additional image with a black cover changes the light
arriving at the camera-side surface; and exposure or camera processing may differ between
captures. These effects are particularly relevant for automotive tinted glass, where
transmission is attenuated and colored before the camera records it. The desired output
is the transmission \emph{as observed through the glass}, not the unfiltered scene behind
the window.

We examine two ambiguities that arise in such pairs. The first is \emph{local}: a
camera-side occluder, such as the photographer, reduces part of the reflected
illumination and can also alter the light reaching the transmitted scene. The resulting
occlusion region $\Omega$ should not be confused with a reflection-location map.
The former describes where an occluder changes the imaging process; the latter
describes where reflected content is visible. A location may belong to one without
belonging to the other. In 82 visually screened pairs, the mean reference intensity
within algorithmically proposed occlusion regions is $0.963$ of that outside.
The inside/outside ratio for $r=I/T$ is $0.832$. Since $r$ includes
transmitted as well as reflected content, these statistics cannot
separately identify illumination and reflection losses. They nevertheless
reveal a photometric difference that a global correction may not describe.
The masks are not pixel-accurate ground truth and may select darker scene content.

The second ambiguity is \emph{global}. In the additive model, changing the scale of the
transmitted layer and compensating in the residual can leave the observed image
unchanged. Thus the absolute transmission scale cannot be inferred from one frame
without further restrictions on the residual or an external photometric reference.
This is a property of the inverse problem, not a claim that an image cannot provide
useful statistical cues about exposure or transmission. Indeed, our experiments
find a systematic brightness bias even when the spatial restoration is useful.

These observations motivate a question about supervision rather than a new backbone:
\emph{does a carefully localized occlusion loss improve restoration, or does its benefit
come from another aspect of the training objective?} We test this question with
matched fine-tuning runs, alternative region placements, reflection-location labels,
and a separate intervention on photometric conditioning. The results caution
against attributing improvements to the most visually distinctive component of a
training procedure. On our 15-scene evaluation panel, a domain-adapted model reaches
$22.70$~dB, compared with $20.50$~dB for the strongest evaluated public method.
The pool with fewer candidate occlusion examples performs better. In a three-seed comparison,
an area-matched random region also performs at least as well as the annotated
occlusion region.

Our contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We assemble a real-pair tinted-glass benchmark with 631 paired observations,
  controlled attenuation variants, a frozen evaluation panel, and explicit
  pair-level-screened candidate masks for camera-side occlusion. We report acquisition
  limitations alongside zero-shot and fine-tuned results
  (Sections~\ref{sec:data}--\ref{sec:ft}).
  \item We distinguish camera-side occlusion from reflection location and formulate
  the resulting local photometric discrepancy. We also characterize the
  ambiguity of the global transmission scale under a single-image mixture model
  (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item We isolate supervision placement in controlled comparisons. The results
  support paired-residual-normalized reconstruction but do not show an additional
  benefit from using the candidate occlusion mask rather than an area-matched random
  one (Section~\ref{sec:diag}).
  \item We evaluate reflection-location supervision and a perturbation-based
  test of photometric conditioning. Better location labels do not, by
  themselves, improve restoration when used as loss weights
  (Sections~\ref{sec:gate} and~\ref{sec:photom}).
\end{itemize}

Our aim is to establish which conclusions these experiments support, including
the negative results, rather than to claim a new network or state-of-the-art
performance on established public benchmarks.
