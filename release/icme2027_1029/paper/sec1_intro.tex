\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A photograph taken through glass contains both the scene behind the
surface and light reflected toward the camera. Recovering the transmitted
scene from a single image is underdetermined, and modern reflection
removal systems learn the separation from paired observations
\cite{li2020ibcln,hu2023dsrnet,zhao2025rdnet}. The quality of these
observations depends on the capture procedure as much as on the number
of training examples. Recent real-image benchmarks have therefore
placed increasing emphasis on data acquisition and reflection
localization~\cite{zhu2024rrw,openrr2025}.

Automotive tinted glass introduces a particular form of mismatch.
Tint attenuates and colors the transmitted scene, while camera-side
objects generate structured reflections. To obtain a reference,
the reflecting side may be covered with a dark material. This
procedure suppresses reflected content and changes the local
lighting conditions during capture. Moreover, a sequence of input
frames can share a single transmission reference, introducing
temporal differences between the two images. The learning target
in our study is the scene \emph{as transmitted through the tinted
glass}, including its tint and attenuation.

These acquisition effects connect two distinct spatial quantities.
Camera-side \emph{occlusion} describes where an object modifies the
reflected light field. \emph{Reflection location} describes where
reflected content is visible in the observed image. A reflected
tree can extend across a window without a corresponding occluder;
conversely, an occluder can produce a weak or dark reflection.
Both phenomena affect paired supervision, but they imply different
forms of spatial guidance. In 82 screened image pairs, the mean
reference-intensity ratio between candidate occlusion regions and
their complements is $0.963$. The corresponding normalized
mixture-to-reference ratio is $0.832$. These measurements establish
a substantial difference in local image statistics and motivate
an explicit comparison of spatial loss supports.

A second issue concerns global appearance. In an additive image
model, a change in transmission scale can be compensated by the
reflection residual while preserving the input. The ambiguity
persists when the desired output includes the tint of the glass.
It is therefore useful to separate spatial restoration accuracy
from photometric calibration and to test the actual response of
a model to any supplied photometric condition.

We study these questions using the same RDNet backbone across
matched fine-tuning runs. The experiments vary the acquisition
domain of real training pairs, the presence of a paired-residual
region loss, the placement of that loss, and the type of
reflection-location supervision. On the frozen 15-scene panel,
the best-matched data pool reaches $22.70$~dB, compared with
$20.50$~dB for the strongest zero-shot method. Across three
seeds, an area-matched random disk achieves $19.90$~dB,
compared with $19.41$~dB for the candidate occlusion mask.
These comparisons separate the effectiveness of a relative-error
objective from the information carried by its spatial support.

Our study makes three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We characterize paired supervision through tinted automotive
  glass using 631 frame--reference pairings, a frozen evaluation
  panel, and measurements of local photometric differences
  (Section~\ref{sec:data}).
  \item We distinguish candidate occlusion regions, reflection
  locations, and the global transmission scale, and formulate
  their roles in the paired training objective
  (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item We conduct matched comparisons of training domains,
  region-loss placement, reflection-location proxies, and
  photometric conditioning. The results quantify their
  respective effects on restoration
  (Sections~\ref{sec:ft}--\ref{sec:photom}).
\end{itemize}
