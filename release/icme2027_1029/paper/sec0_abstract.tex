\begin{abstract}
Paired photographs provide direct supervision for single-image reflection removal (SIRR), but the reference image does not always isolate the transmission observed in the input. We study this problem for automotive tinted glass, where a camera-side occluder suppresses reflected light while also changing local illumination. In 82 annotated image pairs, the measured transmission reference is $3.7\%$ darker inside the occlusion region, whereas the reflected content is $16.8\%$ weaker. The measured input-to-target ratio also falls below one in that region. We formulate the resulting local photometric discrepancy and show why the global transmission scale cannot be uniquely recovered from a single image without additional assumptions.
We then investigate which supervision signals are useful in practice. Our tinted-glass benchmark contains 631 real pairs with controlled attenuation variants and a frozen evaluation panel. Fine-tuning on the better-matched training pool improves PSNR by $2.22$~dB over the strongest evaluated public method, with gains on all 15 evaluation scenes. This improvement, however, cannot be attributed to occlusion localization: across three seeds, a true occlusion mask achieves $19.41$~dB, compared with $19.90$~dB for an area-matched random region. A whole-image reflection-normalized objective reaches $20.05$~dB in a separate screening run. We further find that more accurate reflection-location labels do not improve restoration when used only as loss weights. Together, these results distinguish the benefits of domain-matched paired data and error normalization from those of occlusion-specific supervision.
\end{abstract}
